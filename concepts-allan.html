03 · before it ever lists

Finding cars that haven't hit the market yet

The only way to beat an auction is to not be in one. Three feeds where a car is visible before it is biddable — none of them require anyone's permission:

signal 1

Auction-house catalogues

RM, Gooding, Bonhams and Mecum publish consignment catalogues weeks before the block. A car appearing there in September is knowable in August, and its owner is reachable.

signal 2

Dealer "just acquired" inventory

Dealers post cars to their own sites before syndication picks them up. That gap is days to weeks, and it is where a private deal gets done.

signal 3

The one-appearance money

649 accounts in four months bid $250k+ while appearing on two lots or fewer. Whether that's a plant or a serious private buyer, it is the population worth knowing — and it is knowable.

Everything above runs on public pages and published catalogues. No permission, no grey area, no dependency on a platform that could switch us off.
05 · verifying a car actually changed hands

We found the free route. Buying title data is the fallback, not the plan.

You asked whether the winning bid ever becomes a real transfer. Carfax blocks automated access to every VIN path, so we went looking for what is actually open. Four sources, verified live this week:

free tier · verified

Dealer-listing history by VIN

auto.dev returns VIN-parameter listing search on a genuinely free tier (1,000 calls/month). Marketcheck's VIN-history endpoint returns six years of online listing history per VIN — every dealer, date, price and odometer change. A VIN reappearing in dealer inventory after the hammer is a definitive hands-changed signal, and the price and mileage deltas feed the re-trade metric directly.

official APIs · no scraping · no permission problem
free · robots-permitted

Auction-house archives

Gooding's robots file explicitly permits crawlers and its pages return clean JSON with chassis numbers. Bonhams and Mecum are permitted; RM Sotheby's publishes no restriction at all. A car of yours re-crossing another block is the single highest-value transfer event we can observe.

Barrett-Jackson disallows · we respect it
free · official

State title portals

Florida's MVCheck and Wisconsin's lien search both return title issue date, brands and liens by VIN, free, with no owner names. Low volume, but authoritative corroboration when a specific car matters.

the fallback

Licensed title data

NMVTIS resellers remain available at roughly $1-12 a car if the free routes leave a gap on a specific chassis. We would rather spend that on the two or three cars where it decides something than on a blanket pull.

04 · the Carfax answer you asked for

We can't scrape it, and here's the straight reason

You asked for a Carfax monitor on the GT2 RS to prove whether the title actually moves. We tried. Carfax's own robots file blocks every VIN path — /vehicle/, /vin=, /vehiclehistory/, /api/, /search. classic.com, Cars & Bids and cars.com all return 403 to any automated client.

why we won't route around it

The corpus is only worth what its provenance is worth

Every byte we hold came from a permitted surface. The first question anyone serious asks about a finding is how the data was obtained. We are not trading that for a few hundred dollars of title data — it would make every number above unusable in the exact moment it matters.

what we do instead

Licensed title data, ~$1–12 a car

NMVTIS is the federal title system; approved resellers sell API access legally. Same answer, bought instead of taken. A 100-car pilot is roughly $100–1,200 and tells us whether inflated sales actually transfer. That is a purchase decision, not a technical one.
